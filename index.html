5

Writing workshop: Create your event 🎨

Work in pairs. Plan an event in your area, then turn the information into a clear poster.

Step 1 — agree on an event

Choose a concert, festival, sports event, race, cooking/dance/singing competition, art show, or invent something fun. Make it realistic enough that classmates could imagine attending.

  1. Choose the event. Give it a memorable name and decide what people can do there.
  2. Complete every information field. Decide the place, date, start/end times, ticket options, rules, and a recommendation.
  3. Check the facts. Can a visitor answer “When? Where? How much? What can I do? What are the rules?”
  4. Design the poster. Make the name and most important details easy to see. Use short phrases, not a long paragraph.
  5. Prepare a 30–45 second presentation. Both partners must speak.

Event planning form

Why present before the final conversation?Sharing the poster helps the class discover the events. Listen to your classmates: in the last task, you will be assigned one event and invite a partner to it. The more you understand the posters, the more natural your conversation will be.

Final mission: From interests to an invitation 🗣️

Work in pairs. Use a randomly assigned event and create a conversation that brings Unit 3 together.

Your goal

Have a natural conversation of about 10–14 turns. Talk about free-time activities and preferences first. Then introduce, explore, recommend, and respond to an event. Don't simply read the poster aloud.

Step 2 — build the conversation in this order

  1. Open naturally. Greet your partner and ask what they like doing in their free time.
  2. Explore interests. Ask about music, movies, games, sports, festivals, or other activities. Ask at least one follow-up question.
  3. Compare preferences. React naturally: “Me too!”, “Not really,” “I prefer…”, “That sounds fun!”
  4. Introduce the event. Connect it to what your partner likes: “Speaking of music, there's a…” or “You said you like… Have you heard about…?”
  5. Ask for details. Ask at least three questions about date, time, place, activities, ticket price, or rules.
  6. Recommend and decide. One person gives a recommendation and reason. The other accepts, refuses politely, or asks for more information.
  7. Close the conversation. Agree on a plan or explain why you can't go. End naturally.

Step 3 — example conversation (use it as a model, not a script to copy)

A: Hey! What do you like doing in your free time?

B: I like listening to music and going out with friends. What about you?

A: I love watching movies, but I prefer live music. What kind of music do you like?

B: I like pop and rock. Do you like rock concerts?

A: Yes, I do! Actually, there's a Rock & Pop Night this Saturday.

B: Really? Where is it?

A: It's at City Park. It starts at five and ends at nine.

B: Cool! How much are the tickets?

A: They're eight dollars online or ten dollars at the door. Drinks are OK, but pets aren't allowed.

B: That sounds fun! Who's playing?

A: There are several local bands. I recommend it because the music is great and we can hang out together.

B: I'd love to go! Let's buy the tickets online.

A: Great! Let's meet at the entrance at 4:45.

Notice the order: interests → preferences → event → questions → recommendation → decision and plan.
